# LinkedIn profile outreach

Turn a thoughtful profile review into an initial recruiting message that sounds like a person wrote it. Preserve Rotimi's writing preferences while allowing another recruiter to supply their own name, contact details, and opportunity. This skill is self-contained. It does not require Paraform, another skill, a candidate database, or a specific browser tool.

## Inputs and scope

Use the profile material and opportunity information the user provides. A short description of the work and the relevant selling points is enough; a full requisition is not required. Follow the user's latest instructions when they change the voice, format, or company-disclosure preference.

- If only a URL is supplied, read it with available authorized tools. If the profile cannot be accessed, ask for pasted text or screenshots. Never imply that you reviewed a page you could not read.
- If opportunity details are missing, complete the profile review and identify the strongest hook. Ask for the role's core work and a confirmed selling point before writing a role-specific pitch. If the user wants general networking outreach, write that without inventing an opening.
- Treat missing profile sections as unavailable evidence, not proof that the candidate lacks experience.
- This is a review-and-draft workflow. It does not send messages, save profiles, create projects, update ledgers, or initiate follow-ups.

## Evaluate the profile before writing

Read enough of the full available profile to understand what the person has actually done. Search-result previews can support initial triage but rarely support a distinctive final message.

Separate three questions:

1. **What is established?** Identify the current role, relevant responsibilities, accomplishments, employer-level tenure, progression, and stated professional preferences. Use education only where it adds relevant context and verify the actual education entry.
2. **Why might this opportunity fit?** Match specific work, ownership, career direction, or expressed preferences to the supplied opportunity. Distinguish direct evidence from a plausible inference. Employer prestige, a title, or a school does not prove scope or ability.
3. **What is worth mentioning?** Choose the detail that can start a natural conversation. The strongest assessment evidence is not always the best opening line.

Read career history accurately. Same-employer promotions are progression, not separate job changes. Distinguish full-time employment from internships and explicitly labeled contract work. Do not infer employment type from an employer's name. Do not inflate years of experience or infer age from graduation dates.

Flag material mismatches with the supplied role, such as level, core work, or location. Do not use persuasive writing to disguise a known mismatch. Generic Open to Work status does not establish relocation intent, willingness to take a lower level, or interest in this particular role. If a critical fit question remains unresolved, label it clearly; a draft is not a qualification or contactability decision.

Apply only selection criteria supplied for the task. Do not import historical client exclusions, school rankings, arbitrary tenure thresholds, or other campaign rules into this writing workflow. Never infer protected traits, nationality, citizenship, work authorization, personality, or private motivations from names, photographs, schools, or locations.

## Find one non-obvious professional hook

Review the available sources in this order, choosing relevance over novelty:

1. Stated next-step preferences: target work, role family, workplace type, or timing.
2. Recommendations: a visible working relationship or specific observation that connects to the career story.
3. About/summary: distinctive language about what the person builds, wants to work on, or values professionally.
4. Accomplishments and projects: a concrete launch, publication, portfolio project, or other relevant work.
5. Career pattern: a promotion path, thoughtful transition, repeated specialty, or unusual combination of experience.
6. Location or workplace alignment when it matters to the opportunity.
7. Public professional volunteer work when clearly relevant and not sensitive.

Do not personalize from LinkedIn Skills, endorsements, assessments, or Interests. Do not use sensitive personal details or protected traits. Avoid generic praise such as “impressive background” or “your experience really stood out.”

Notice the story instead of mining a flattering quote. For example, a recommendation from an internship manager followed by several years of growth at the same company gives a natural observation. It does not justify claims that the candidate is loyal, unusually driven, or endorsed for this opportunity.

Mention a recommender's name only when the relationship is visible and relevant. Never imply that you know the recommender, spoke with them, or received a referral unless the user supplies that fact.

Keep the hook at conversational detail: one name, one career turn, one stated preference, or one concrete piece of work. Use at most two connected signals. Do not stack employers, credentials, dates, and metrics to demonstrate how much research you did.

If the profile is sparse, use one modest, factual work observation. If no useful detail is available, say that personalization is limited. Do not manufacture a story.

## Connect the observation to the opportunity

Select one or two confirmed facts that matter to this person: the work itself, ownership, customer exposure, team scope, location, or another supplied selling point. Explain the connection plainly without narrating your evaluation.

- Default to withholding the client company's name in the initial message unless the user asks to include it. Describe the opportunity concretely enough to be useful; avoid mystery-client hype.
- Use only supported role facts. Do not invent funding, compensation, equity, benefits, flexibility, urgency, or hiring-manager interest.
- Preserve distinctions such as base pay versus total compensation and remote versus hybrid. If details conflict, omit the disputed claim and flag it for the user.
- Do not expose confidential hiring notes, rejection feedback, or internal recruiter context.
- Avoid “that made me keep reading,” “that was a good reason to reach out,” “that proves you're committed,” or “you're a perfect fit.” State the observation, introduce the work, and let the connection land.

## Write in Rotimi's voice

Warm, direct, lightly conversational, and specific. Use the candidate's first name and natural contractions. Sound confident without exaggerated praise, forced familiarity, or a sales pitch.

For a normal LinkedIn message or InMail, use this shape flexibly:

```text
Hi FirstName,

[One or two short sentences about a specific professional observation.]

[Two or three short sentences about the opportunity and its most relevant confirmed selling point.]

[One simple question inviting a conversation.]

[Optional warm stay-in-touch close.]
```

Writing preferences:

- No em dashes. Use periods, commas, parentheses, or simple hyphens.
- Keep sentences short, with no more than one comma per sentence. Avoid three-part lists and dense paragraphs.
- Put a blank line after the greeting and between paragraphs.
- Use plain words. Cut corporate filler, vague impact claims, “unique blend,” “at the intersection of,” and “not just X but Y” constructions.
- Do not recite the résumé or explain what the profile supposedly proves about the person's character.
- Keep one clear ask. Do not demand a résumé, availability, and a call in the same first message.
- Add a subject only when requested or the channel needs one. Keep it brief and factual.
- For a connection note or supplied character limit, compress to the observation and a simple ask. Drop the long closing. Do not assume a platform character limit that has not been established.

Rotimi's established longer close is:

```text
Even if you're not interested, I'd love to stay in touch for future roles. Feel free to save my email. If you could reply either way, I'd really appreciate it 😅
```

Use it when it suits the requested length and tone. Include “Feel free to save my email” only when the sender's verified email is supplied and visible in the message or signature. Otherwise omit that sentence. Do not insert Rotimi's identity, email, calendar, or company into another recruiter's message. The emoji and longer close are defaults that the user can change, not mandatory elements of every draft.

## Output and final check

Unless the user requests message-only output, provide a compact profile read, the selected hook, any material uncertainty, and one paste-ready draft. Keep analysis outside the candidate-facing text. Offer multiple versions only when asked or when a real tone choice warrants it.

Before delivering:

- Trace each candidate claim to the supplied or observed profile evidence and each opportunity claim to the brief.
- Check that all details belong to this candidate and this opportunity. Never carry a hook, name, or selling point across candidates by accident.
- Ensure the hook adds something beyond a job-title match and contains no invented familiarity or personality claim.
- Read the message aloud mentally. Remove any sentence that sounds like a brochure, a profile recap, or internal recruiting analysis.
- Check the name, punctuation, supplied length limit, and absence of placeholders in a sendable draft.
- Label missing inputs honestly. Do not describe a draft as sent or a candidate as verified contactable.

## Fictional calibration example

**Profile evidence:** Maya interned at Northline, returned as a full-time engineer, and later became a senior engineer there. A visible recommendation is from Jenna, identified as her internship manager. The profile describes Maya's work on customer-facing workflow tools.

**Opportunity facts:** Senior product engineer at a small software company. Own a customer-facing workflow from design through launch and speak directly with customers. The sender's email is not supplied.

**Profile read:** Her workflow-tool experience supports a relevant connection. The return and progression at Northline provide a more personal opening. Location, work preferences, and overall role fit remain unverified.

**Draft:**

```text
Hi Maya,

I saw Jenna's recommendation from your Northline internship. It was cool to see that you came back and kept growing there.

I'm helping a small software company hire a senior product engineer. You'd own a customer-facing workflow from design through launch and work directly with customers. Your work on workflow tools seemed relevant.

Would you be open to a quick conversation?

Even if you're not interested, I'd love to stay in touch for future roles. If you could reply either way, I'd really appreciate it 😅
```

The facts make this example work. Do not reuse its recommendation or career-arc opening when another profile does not support it.
